Local convergence

Close to a stationary point, Newton's method converges quadratically: the error is squared at every step, so the number of correct digits roughly doubles. The catch is the word "close".

The theorem

The statement measures matrices with the operator norm, the most a matrix can stretch a unit vector:

Definition (operator norm of a matrix)
\[ \|M\| = \max\big\{ \|Mx\| : \|x\| = 1 \big\}. \]
Theorem (local convergence of Newton's method)

Let \(f\) be twice continuously differentiable with \(\nabla f(x^*) = 0\). Assume that

  • \(\big\|\big(\nabla^2 f(x^*)\big)^{-1}\big\| \le \tfrac1m\) for some \(m > 0\), and
  • \(\nabla^2 f\) is \(L\)-Lipschitz in the \(\beta\)-ball around \(x^*\): \(\|\nabla^2 f(x) - \nabla^2 f(y)\| \le L\|x - y\|\) for all \(x, y \in \mathcal{B}(x^*, \beta)\).

Define \(\delta = \min\big\{\beta, \tfrac{2m}{3L}\big\}\). If \(\|x_0 - x^*\| \le \delta\), then for all \(k = 0, 1, 2, \dots\)

\[ \|x_{k+1} - x^*\| \le \delta \qquad\text{and}\qquad \|x_{k+1} - x^*\| \le \frac{3L}{2m}\,\|x_k - x^*\|^2. \]

The two assumptions in words. The Hessian at \(x^*\) is invertible, and not nearly singular (its inverse is bounded by \(1/m\)). And the Hessian does not change too fast near \(x^*\).

Corollary
\[ \|x_k - x^*\| \le \frac1C\big(C\|x_0 - x^*\|\big)^{2^k}, \qquad C = \frac{3L}{2m}. \]

Apply the theorem \(k\) times. With \(e_k = C\|x_k - x^*\|\), the theorem says \(e_{k+1} \le e_k^2\), so \(e_k \le e_0^{2^k}\). A start in the \(\delta\)-ball gives \(e_0 \le C\delta \le 1\). Whenever \(e_0 < 1\), raising it to the power \(2^k\) makes the error collapse.

What it says

  • Once in the \(\delta\)-neighbourhood of \(x^*\), the algorithm stays there.
  • Quadratic convergence inside it: \(\dfrac{\|x_{k+1} - x^*\|}{\|x_k - x^*\|^2} \le \dfrac{3L}{2m}\). In the language of class 18, the order is \(p = 2\).
  • Accuracy \(\varepsilon\) after \(O\big(\log\log\tfrac1\varepsilon\big)\) iterations. The exponent \(2^k\) is why the logarithm appears twice. In practice this means a handful of iterations, whatever the tolerance.
  • Attracted to local minima and maxima. The theorem only needs \(\nabla f(x^*) = 0\). A maximum with an invertible Hessian attracts the iterates just as well as a minimum.
  • \(\beta\), \(m\) and \(L\) are hard to estimate, but the algorithm never uses them. They only appear in the analysis.
  • No convexity needed. The algorithm and its local convergence only require that \(H(x^*) = \nabla^2 f(x^*)\) is nonsingular and not badly behaved near \(x^*\).

The proof

Write \(g(x) = \nabla f(x)\) and \(H(x) = \nabla^2 f(x)\). The lecture uses two lemmas and states them without proof in the main slides.

Lemma 1 (from the Hessian to the gradient)
\[ g(x_k) - g(x^*) = \int_0^1 H\big(x^* + t(x_k - x^*)\big)(x_k - x^*)\,dt. \]
Lemma 2 (the inverse Hessian stays bounded)

Under the conditions of the theorem, \(\|H(x)^{-1}\| \le \dfrac{1}{m - L\|x - x^*\|}\) for all \(x \in \mathcal{B}(x^*, \beta)\).

Start from the definition of \(x_{k+1}\) and use \(g(x^*) = 0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} x_{k+1} - x^* &= x_k - x^* - H(x_k)^{-1}\big(g(x_k) - g(x^*)\big) \\ &= (x_k - x^*) - H(x_k)^{-1}\int_0^1 H\big(x^* + t(x_k - x^*)\big)(x_k - x^*)\,dt \quad \text{(Lemma 1)} \\ &= H(x_k)^{-1}\int_0^1 \Big[H(x_k) - H\big(x^* + t(x_k - x^*)\big)\Big](x_k - x^*)\,dt. \end{aligned} \]

The new error is the old error multiplied by a difference of two Hessians. If the Hessian changes slowly, that difference is small.

Take norms. Lemma 2 bounds \(\|H(x_k)^{-1}\|\). The Lipschitz condition bounds the Hessian difference, since \(x_k\) and \(x^* + t(x_k - x^*)\) are \((1 - t)\|x_k - x^*\|\) apart:

\[ \|x_{k+1} - x^*\| \le \frac{1}{m - L\|x_k - x^*\|}\int_0^1 L(1 - t)\|x_k - x^*\|^2\,dt = \frac{L}{2\big(m - L\|x_k - x^*\|\big)}\|x_k - x^*\|^2. \]

Inside the \(\delta\)-ball, \(L\|x_k - x^*\| \le 2m/3\), so the denominator is at least \(2(m - 2m/3) = 2m/3\). Two conclusions follow:

  1. \(\|x_{k+1} - x^*\| \le \dfrac{2m/3}{2m/3}\|x_k - x^*\| = \|x_k - x^*\| \le \delta\). The iterates stay in the ball.
  2. \(\|x_{k+1} - x^*\| \le \dfrac{L}{2m/3}\|x_k - x^*\|^2 = \dfrac{3L}{2m}\|x_k - x^*\|^2\). Quadratic convergence.

Conclusion 1 is what makes induction work: the next iterate is again in the ball, so the bound applies again.

A practice exam problem

The Fall 2025 practice exam asks: minimize \(f(x) = 6x - \log x\) over \(x > 0\) with pure Newton. What happens from \(x_0 = 0.2\), and from \(x_0 = 0.4\)?

  1. \(f'(x) = 6 - 1/x\) and \(f''(x) = 1/x^2 > 0\), so \(f\) is convex.
  2. \(f'(x) = 0\) gives the global minimum \(x^* = 1/6\).
  3. The Newton step: \(x_{k+1} = x_k - x_k^2(6 - 1/x_k) = 2x_k - 6x_k^2\).
  4. The error: \(x_{k+1} - \tfrac16 = -6\big(x_k - \tfrac16\big)^2\). So \(\dfrac{x_{k+1} - x^*}{x_k - x^*} = 1 - 6x_k\).

From \(x_0 = 0.2\), the ratio is \(|1 - 1.2| = 0.2 < 1\), the error shrinks, and it keeps shrinking faster. The iterates are 0.16, 0.1664, 0.16666 and so on. From \(x_0 = 0.4\), the ratio is \(|1 - 2.4| = 1.4 > 1\). The first step lands at \(x_1 = -0.16\), outside the domain, and the iterates diverge.

Step 4 is the theorem in miniature. The error is squared at every step, with constant 6. It shrinks only if \(6|x_0 - x^*| < 1\), that is \(0 < x_0 < 1/3\). That interval is the neighbourhood where pure Newton works. Try both starts in the first visual of part a.

Newton against gradient descent

The lecture's example lives on the triangle \(x_1, x_2 > 0\), \(x_1 + x_2 < 1\):

\[ f(x_1, x_2) = -\log(1 - x_1 - x_2) - \log x_1 - \log x_2. \]
\[ \nabla f = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{1 - x_1 - x_2} - \frac{1}{x_1} \\ \frac{1}{1 - x_1 - x_2} - \frac{1}{x_2} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \nabla^2 f = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{(1 - x_1 - x_2)^2} + \frac{1}{x_1^2} & \frac{1}{(1 - x_1 - x_2)^2} \\ \frac{1}{(1 - x_1 - x_2)^2} & \frac{1}{(1 - x_1 - x_2)^2} + \frac{1}{x_2^2} \end{pmatrix}. \]

By symmetry the minimum is at \(x^* = (1/3, 1/3)\).

Summary

  • If \(\nabla f(x^*) = 0\), \(\|\nabla^2 f(x^*)^{-1}\| \le 1/m\) and the Hessian is \(L\)-Lipschitz near \(x^*\), then from any start within \(\delta = \min\{\beta, 2m/(3L)\}\): \(\|x_{k+1} - x^*\| \le \frac{3L}{2m}\|x_k - x^*\|^2\).
  • Quadratic convergence, \(O(\log\log(1/\varepsilon))\) iterations, but only locally.
  • No convexity needed, and maxima attract as well as minima.
How this shows up on the exam

Last year's midterm ranked gradient descent, Newton's method and stochastic gradient descent on a regression problem. Cost per iteration, from cheapest: SGD, gradient descent, Newton. Number of iterations, from fewest: Newton, gradient descent, SGD. It then showed three contour plots of iterates: the one with very few, direct steps is Newton's method.

The practice exam problem above, \(f(x) = 6x - \log x\), is the other classic: compute the error ratio, then decide convergence from two starting points.

Check yourself
Quick check
Newton's method is close to \(x^*\) with \(\|x_k - x^*\| = 10^{-3}\) and \(\frac{3L}{2m} = 1\). What does the theorem guarantee for \(\|x_{k+2} - x^*\|\)?
One step gives at most \((10^{-3})^2 = 10^{-6}\). The next gives at most \((10^{-6})^2 = 10^{-12}\). The exponent doubles at every step.
Quick check
\(f\) is not convex. \(x^*\) is a local maximum with \(\nabla^2 f(x^*)\) invertible, and the Hessian is Lipschitz near \(x^*\). Pure Newton starts very close to \(x^*\). What happens?
The theorem only needs \(\nabla f(x^*) = 0\), an invertible Hessian and a Lipschitz Hessian. Newton solves \(\nabla f = 0\), so a nearby maximum attracts it.
