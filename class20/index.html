Class 20 · Non-linear optimization

Newton's method

Use the curvature as well as the slope. Newton's method replaces the function by its quadratic approximation and jumps to that quadratic's minimum. Near the optimum it is astonishingly fast. Far away it needs a safety net.

Overview

The big picture

Class 18 walked downhill along the gradient. Class 19 made each step cheaper. Class 20 goes the other way: each step costs more, because it uses second derivatives, and in return far fewer steps are needed.

\[ x_{k+1} = x_k - \big(\nabla^2 f(x_k)\big)^{-1}\nabla f(x_k) \]

Gradient descent is a hiker in fog who can only feel which way the ground slopes, and takes a cautious step that way. Newton's method also feels how the slope is changing. From that it pictures the valley as a bowl, works out where the bottom of the bowl is, and jumps there. If the bowl is a good picture of the valley, one or two jumps are enough. If it is not, the jump can land somewhere strange.

Second-order view: minimize the second-order Taylor approximation of \(f\). First-order view: find the root of the first-order Taylor approximation of \(\nabla f\). Both give the same step. The first view is the original one: Newton and Raphson built the method in the 1600s to solve equations.

Each iteration needs the Hessian and a linear solve, \(O(n^3)\) operations. In return, close to the optimum the error is squared at every step. On the lecture's example in part b, Newton needs 6 iterations where gradient descent with a constant step needs over a hundred. But pure Newton is only guaranteed to work near the optimum, and it is attracted to maxima as readily as to minima.

The story of the class in three steps

20a · StepThe Newton direction, the decrement, logistic regression.
20b · LocalQuadratic convergence near the optimum. No convexity needed.
20c · GlobalA step size and strong convexity: a damped phase, then a local phase.
Four takeaways from the lecture
  1. Newton's method was first developed to find roots of equations, then extended to optimization.
  2. It augments descent methods with second-order (Hessian) information.
  3. It converges very fast (quadratically) locally. So it works in two steps: get near a stationary point, then find it, exploiting quadratic convergence.
  4. Numerous enhancements exist to address its global convergence issues.

Exam practice

Test yourself

Multiple choice in the style of the midterm. Say your one-line justification before you click.

Question 1 · Descent
Newton's method with step size 1 is a descent algorithm. True or false?
Newton's method can be attracted by a local maximum, and the full step from the quadratic model can be too large. Nothing guarantees \(f(x_{k+1}) \le f(x_k)\).
Question 2 · One dimension
Minimize \(f(x) = 6x - \log x\) over \(x > 0\) with pure Newton. Which starting points give convergence to \(x^* = 1/6\)?
The update is \(x_{k+1} = 2x_k - 6x_k^2\), so \(x_{k+1} - \tfrac16 = -6(x_k - \tfrac16)^2\) and the error ratio is \(|1 - 6x_k|\). The error shrinks if and only if \(6|x_0 - \tfrac16| < 1\). From \(x_0 = 0.4\) the first step lands at \(-0.16\). Convexity does not save pure Newton.
Question 3 · Ranking
On a large regression problem with the same small step size, rank gradient descent, Newton's method and SGD by the number of iterations, from fewest to most.
Newton uses curvature and needs the fewest iterations. SGD uses noisy one-point gradients and needs the most. The cost per iteration ranks the other way round.
Question 4 · Quadratics
\(f(x) = \tfrac12 x^\top Qx - c^\top x\) with \(Q \succ 0\). How many pure Newton iterations does it take to reach the minimum?
The second-order model of a quadratic is the quadratic itself. Newton minimizes the model exactly: \(x_1 = x_0 - Q^{-1}(Qx_0 - c) = Q^{-1}c\).
Question 5 · Local convergence
Which assumption does the local convergence theorem for Newton's method not require?
The theorem needs a stationary point, a bounded inverse Hessian at \(x^*\) and a Lipschitz Hessian nearby. Convexity is not needed. Without it, \(x^*\) can be a maximum, and Newton converges to it just as fast.
Question 6 · Global convergence
Under the global convergence theorem, the gradient norm is \(\|\nabla f(x_k)\| = 5\) and \(\eta = 0.1\). Which step size does the theorem prescribe?
The gradient is above \(\eta\), so the method is in the damped phase: \(\alpha_k = m/M\), which guarantees a drop of at least \(\gamma = \frac{m}{2M^2}\eta^2\). Full steps start once \(\|\nabla f(x_k)\| < \eta\).
Question 7 · Logistic regression
Applied to logistic regression, each Newton iteration amounts to:
\(\beta^{k+1} = \arg\min_\beta (z^k - X\beta)^\top W(z^k - X\beta)\), where \(W\) and the adjusted response \(z^k\) depend on \(\beta^k\). Hence iteratively re-weighted least squares.
