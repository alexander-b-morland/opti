Why uncertainty matters

Linear optimization treats every number in the model as exact. Real numbers are estimates. This part shows how badly that can go, and what a better method should deliver.

When the world disagrees with the model

The lecture opens with two engineering stories. The Tacoma Narrows Bridge was designed to withstand 120 mph winds, yet it collapsed in a 42 mph wind because of a resonance effect its designers had not modelled. A schedule for ships passing through the Panama Canal can squeeze in six extra large ships per day, but only if no vessel breaks down and no rope snaps.

The common thread: a design that is optimal for the expected conditions can fail badly under conditions that are only slightly different. Optimization makes this worse, because an optimal solution typically sits right on the edge of the feasible region. Any small error in the data can push it over the edge.

A constraint from PILOT4

NETLIB is a standard library of real linear optimization problems. One constraint of the PILOT4 problem has the form \(a^\top x \ge b\) with about thirty coefficients, such as \(-15.79081\), \(-8.598819\), \(717.562256\), and a right-hand side \(b = 23.387405\).

Some coefficients, like 1, are clearly exact. Others, like 8.598819, are estimates and probably not accurate to the last digit. Two questions follow. Will the nominal solution \(x^*\) stay optimal if the data are slightly off? Will it even stay feasible?

Suppose every uncertain coefficient may be off by 0.1%: \(|a_i - \bar a_i| \le 0.001\,|\bar a_i|\). How badly can the nominal solution violate the constraint?

\[ \min_{a}\, (a^\top x^* - b) = -128.8 \approx -5.5\,b. \]

A 0.1% error in the data can produce a constraint violation of 550%.

Now let each coefficient be \(a_i = \bar a_i + \varepsilon_i |\bar a_i|\) with \(\varepsilon_i\) uniform on \([-0.001, 0.001]\), and simulate 1,000 samples. Measure the relative violation \(V = \max\big[(b - a^\top x^*)/|b|,\ 0\big]\).

  • The constraint is violated in 50% of the samples.
  • The average violation is 125%: \(\mathbb{E}(V) = 1.25\).
  • The worst sampled violation is 450%.

With only 0.01% uncertainty in the parameters, 19 of the 90 NETLIB problems have constraints violated by more than 5%, and 13 of the 90 have constraints violated by more than 50%.

So this is not a quirk of one problem. Nominal solutions of real models are fragile as a rule.

The core problem

Linear optimization pushes the solution to a vertex, where constraints hold with equality. Any error in the data then decides, by chance, whether the solution is feasible. Being optimal and being fragile go together.

Why not just use probability?

The obvious approach is to model each uncertain number as a random variable and optimize, for example, the probability of staying feasible. The lecture lists four reasons this is hard in practice:

  • Data. Probability distributions are rarely known. You have a mean and a standard deviation at best.
  • Analysis. The probability that a constraint is violated is hard to write down as a function of \(x\).
  • Computation. Simulation only approximates these probabilities, and can be expensive.
  • Scale. All of this gets worse quickly as the number of variables and constraints grows.

What robust optimization promises

Robust optimization sets itself two goals:

  1. Solutions that stay feasible and perform well even when the parameter estimates are wrong.
  2. A method that stays tractable in high dimensions, so it works on problems as large as the nominal ones.

The next part shows how: replace "the data equal their estimates" by "the data lie somewhere in a set", and require the solution to work for every point in that set.
