Building uncertainty sets

The uncertainty set is the main modeling choice in robust optimization. Too small and the solution is fragile. Too large and it is needlessly conservative. A worked example shows the trade-off.

Example: capacity expansion

A company can build factories in four new markets with a $500M budget. Each factory earns a long-term net present value (NPV). Construction costs are uncertain, but past projects give their mean and standard deviation.

Market1234
Expected cost \(\bar a_i\) ($M)120100180140
Standard deviation \(\sigma_i\) ($M)12101814
NPV ($B)50406030

With \(x_i\) the number of factories in market \(i\), the deterministic model is an integer knapsack problem:

\[ \max\ 50x_1 + 40x_2 + 60x_3 + 30x_4 \quad \text{s.t.}\quad 120x_1 + 100x_2 + 180x_3 + 140x_4 \le 500,\quad x \in \mathbb{Z}_+^4. \]

The nominal solution is \(x = (4, 0, 0, 0)\): four factories in market 1, costing $480M with $20M to spare. If costs come in slightly above expectation, the budget is blown. How should we protect against cost overruns?

First attempt: element-wise deviations

Let each cost deviate from its mean by at most \(\Gamma\) standard deviations, for example \(\Gamma = 1, 2, 3\):

\[ \mathcal{U}_1 = \Big\{ a \in \mathbb{R}^4 : -\Gamma \le \frac{a_i - \bar a_i}{\sigma_i} \le \Gamma,\ i = 1, \dots, 4 \Big\}. \]

This is a box. The robust constraint \(a^\top x \le 500\) for all \(a \in \mathcal{U}_1\) is easy to read: with \(x \ge 0\), the worst case puts every cost at its maximum, so the constraint becomes \(\sum_i (\bar a_i + \Gamma\sigma_i)x_i \le 500\).

Fair criticism

The box assumes all costs can hit their worst value at the same time. With independent projects that is very unlikely. The box is safe, but it may be too pessimistic.

Second attempt: the Central Limit Theorem

Reminder: Central Limit Theorem

If \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) are i.i.d. with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\), then as \(n \to \infty\),

\[ \frac{X_1 + \dots + X_n - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1). \]

Intuitively, a sum of independent random variables stays close to its mean: deviations tend to cancel. The lecture builds this into the set by limiting the total normalised deviation:

\[ \mathcal{U}_2 = \Big\{ a \in \mathbb{R}^n : -\Gamma\sqrt{n} \le \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{a_i - \bar a_i}{\sigma_i} \le \Gamma\sqrt{n} \Big\}. \]
  • For \(\Gamma = 2\), \(\mathbb{P}[a \in \mathcal{U}_2] \approx 0.95\) for large \(n\). For \(\Gamma = 3\), about 0.997.
  • \(\Gamma\) still controls how much protection you buy.
The philosophy

Robust optimization does not start from the axioms of probability (distributions, densities). It starts from one of its conclusions, the Central Limit Theorem, and turns it into a set. No distributional assumption is needed beyond a mean and a spread.

What the numbers say

  • More \(\Gamma\), more safety, less value. As \(\Gamma\) grows, the plan keeps more capital unused, is more likely to stay within budget, and earns a lower NPV.
  • The price of robustness can be small. For small \(\Gamma\) the robust plan keeps about 90% of the nominal NPV while raising the chance of staying within budget a lot.
  • The set matters. The CLT set limits the adversary more, so its solutions are less conservative: less unused capital, a slightly lower but similar probability of staying within budget, and a higher NPV.
  • More uncertain parameters, cheaper robustness. The CLT set performs even better as \(n\) grows, because the approximation improves. With 10 variables instead of 4, the price of robustness shrinks.
How to choose \(\Gamma\)?

Treat it like a hyperparameter in machine learning. Solve for several values, evaluate each solution on held-out or simulated data, and pick the trade-off you want.

Case study: nurse scheduling

An emergency department must decide how many nurses of each experience tier \(q\) work in each position \(j\) (clinical leader, triage, a pod of patients), in each shift \(i\), on each day \(d\). The decision variables are \(z_{qjid}\), the number of such nurses. Understaffing hurts patients, overstaffing wastes nurses, and nurses have preferences. Patient demand varies by hour and by day, and is hard to forecast.

The core model minimizes the number of nurse-shifts \(\sum z_{qjid}\) subject to scheduling rules \(z \in \mathcal{Z}\): minimum and maximum staffing, eligibility, week-to-week consistency, available nurses and working-hour limits.

The uncertainty set

Historical data give target staffing levels \(\bar h_{jsw}\) (nurses needed in position \(j\) at hour \(s\) of week \(w\)). The set bounds the forecast error at three levels:

  • for each hour and position, at most \(\Gamma^1_{js}\),
  • summed over a week for each position, at most \(\Gamma^2_j\),
  • summed over a week and all positions, at most \(\Gamma^3\).

This is a polyhedral set, a richer version of the CLT idea: individual errors can be large, but they cannot all be large together.

The robust model and the result

The objective combines three goals with weights \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2\): few nurse-shifts, little insufficiency relative to the target nurse-to-patient ratio (npr, protected for all demand in \(\mathcal{U}\)), and little change from the current schedule.

Varying \(\lambda_1\) traces out the cost versus insufficiency trade-off, and the chosen schedule improves on the current one in both.

Summary

  • The box set protects each parameter separately and is often too conservative.
  • The CLT set bounds the total deviation, because independent errors tend to cancel. It gives less conservative solutions.
  • \(\Gamma\) trades value for protection. Choose it like a hyperparameter.
  • The price of robustness is often small, and shrinks as the number of uncertain parameters grows.
How this shows up on the exam

Expect to write an uncertainty set from a verbal description ("each yield is at least its lower bound, and the total yield is at least \(\Gamma_j\)"), and to explain in words why one set gives a more conservative solution than another.

Quick check
With the box set and \(\Gamma = 1\), which plan is robust feasible for the capacity expansion problem?
Worst-case costs are \(132, 110, 198, 154\). Then \((2, 2)\) costs \(264 + 220 = 484 \le 500\), while \((3, 1)\) costs \(396 + 110 = 506\) and \((4, 0)\) costs \(528\).
