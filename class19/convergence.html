Convergence and step sizes

With a constant step, SGD rushes towards the optimum and then keeps bouncing around it. The two theorems in this part say how fast the first phase is, how large the bouncing is, and how shrinking the step removes it.

Two phases: fast, then noisy

Run SGD with a constant step size on a well-behaved problem and two phases appear:

  1. Linear convergence to a neighbourhood of the optimum. Far away, most data points agree on the rough direction, so the noisy steps still make steady progress.
  2. Persistent fluctuation inside that neighbourhood, the "noise ball". Near the optimum the data points disagree, and the random direction keeps kicking the iterate around.

Gradient descent converges to the stationary point itself, which for a convex problem is the global optimum. SGD gets close, then keeps moving. These noisy patterns can help escape poor local optima in non-convex problems. They also mean you should return the best solution visited, not simply the last one.

The objective-value guarantee

Theorem (convergence in expected objective value)

Let \(f\) be \(M\)-smooth and \(m\)-strongly convex. Suppose the stochastic gradients are unbiased with bounded variance, \(\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f_i(x) - \nabla f(x)\|^2 \le \sigma^2\) for all \(x\). Then SGD with constant step size \(0 < \alpha \le 1/M\) satisfies

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[f(x_k)\big] - z^* \ \le\ \underbrace{(1 - \alpha m)^k \big(f(x_0) - z^*\big)}_{\text{shrinks linearly}} \ +\ \underbrace{\frac{\alpha M \sigma^2}{2m}}_{\text{noise floor}}. \]
  • The first term is the same kind of linear rate as gradient descent in class 18 (with \(\alpha = 1/M\) it is exactly \((1 - m/M)^k\)).
  • The second term does not go to zero. It is the size of the noise ball, and it is proportional to the step size \(\alpha\) and to the gradient noise \(\sigma^2\).
  • So a smaller step gives a smaller noise ball but a slower first phase. That is the central trade-off of SGD.

The gradient guarantee

Without convexity we cannot hope for the optimal value, but we can ask how small the gradient gets.

Theorem (convergence of the gradient)

Let \(f\) be \(M\)-smooth and bounded below by \(z^*\). Sample indices independently and uniformly, and assume \(\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f_i(x)\|^2 \le G^2\) for all \(x\). Then SGD with positive step sizes \(\alpha_\ell\) satisfies, for \(k \ge 1\),

\[ \min_{\ell = 0, \dots, k-1} \mathbb{E}\big\|\nabla f(x_\ell)\big\|^2 \ \le\ \frac{f(x_0) - z^*}{\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\alpha_\ell} \ +\ \frac{MG^2}{2}\, \frac{\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\alpha_\ell^2}{\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\alpha_\ell}. \]

With a constant step \(\alpha_\ell = \alpha\), the right side becomes \(\dfrac{f(x_0) - z^*}{k\alpha} + \dfrac{MG^2\alpha}{2}\), that is \(O(1/k) + O(\alpha)\). Gradient descent achieves \(O(1/k)\) alone. The \(O(\alpha)\) term is SGD's irreducible error for a fixed step.

Constant or diminishing steps

Converges to an approximate solution: fast at first, then stuck in a noise ball whose size grows with \(\alpha\).

Steps such as \(\alpha_k = \alpha/k\) or \(\alpha_k = \alpha/\sqrt{k}\). With these steps, SGD can converge to an exact solution, at the cost of slower convergence. Diminishing step sizes have shown strong practical performance.

Large steps early, to jump to better regions and avoid poor solutions. Small steps later, to refine locally. Diminishing schedules perform well in practice, but finding a good schedule takes experimentation.

The noise ball

With a constant step, the gold gap curve drops quickly and then levels off at a floor while gradient descent keeps going down. Halve \(\alpha\) and the floor drops, but the first phase slows down. Switch to \(\alpha/\sqrt{k+1}\) and the floor keeps sinking, slowly. With \(\alpha/(k+1)\) the steps shrink so fast that the iterates stall long before the optimum: the slower convergence the lecture warns about, in its most extreme form.

Summary

  • Constant step, strongly convex: \(\mathbb{E}f(x_k) - z^* \le (1 - \alpha m)^k(f(x_0) - z^*) + \alpha M\sigma^2/(2m)\). Linear phase plus a noise floor.
  • Smooth, possibly non-convex: \(\min_\ell \mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(x_\ell)\|^2 = O(1/k) + O(\alpha)\) with a constant step.
  • Diminishing steps remove the floor, slowly. Keep the best iterate, not the last.
How this shows up on the exam

Be ready to explain the two terms of the bound in words: the first is the forgetting of the starting point, the second is the price of noisy gradients. And to say what changing \(\alpha\) does to each.

Check yourself
Quick check
You halve the constant step size of SGD on a strongly convex problem. According to the bound, what happens?
The floor \(\alpha M\sigma^2/(2m)\) is proportional to \(\alpha\). The contraction factor \(1 - \alpha m\) moves closer to 1, so the first term shrinks more slowly.
