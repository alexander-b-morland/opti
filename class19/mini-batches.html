Mini-batches and practice

Between one data point and all of them lies a whole range of batch sizes. Mini-batches keep most of SGD's speed and remove much of its noise. This part closes with the lecture's practical checklist.

SGD with mini-batches

Instead of one index, sample a batch \(I_k \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}\) of size \(B\) and average its gradients:

\[ x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha_k \cdot \frac1B \sum_{i \in I_k} \nabla f_i(x_k). \]

The algorithm is the same as before, with steps 2.1 and 2.2 replaced by: choose a batch \(I_k\) with \(|I_k| = B\), and take \(d_k = -\frac1B\sum_{i \in I_k}\nabla f_i(x_k)\).

Why it helps: variance reduction

Averaging \(B\) independent noisy estimates reduces their variance, roughly by a factor \(B\) when the batch is sampled with replacement. A less noisy direction means a smaller noise ball for the same step size.

A continuum from SGD to GD

Batch sizeMethodCharacter
\(B = 1\)SGDFast iterations, noisy, more chances to escape poor solutions
\(1 < B < n\)Mini-batch SGDThe middle ground
\(B = n\)Gradient descentSlow iterations, stable, fewer iterations

In the lecture's regression example, small mini-batches behave like SGD with more stability, and larger ones are more stable still, at the cost of slower iterations. Gradient descent shows the strongest and most stable progress per iteration. SGD makes faster iterations, which can mean faster progress per unit of time, but noisily. Mini-batches can achieve the best of both: faster than gradient descent, steadier than SGD.

Case study: predicting heart disease

A classification problem: predict a heart disease diagnosis from patient attributes.

  • 303 patients, 29 features (demographics such as age, and medical history such as chest pain). 80% for training, 20% for testing.
  • A neural network with one hidden layer of 16 ReLU nodes and a cross-entropy loss.
  • 497 parameters: \(29 \times 16 = 464\) weights into the hidden layer, 16 hidden biases, 16 weights to the output, and 1 output bias.

As in gradient descent, the step size decides everything. Small steps make limited progress. Large steps converge fast or diverge and oscillate. In between, SGD converges well.

Large steps at first, to jump to better regions and avoid poor solutions. Small steps later, to improve locally. Getting the schedule right requires experimentation and fine-tuning.

SGD reaches better solutions than gradient descent here, by avoiding local minima. Mini-batching stabilizes the noisy convergence.

Practical checklist

1 · InitializationA strong starting point speeds up convergence and leads to better solutions. Warm-start from past solves. Try several starts to avoid bad local optima.
2 · BatchingChoose the batch size to trade off the number of iterations against the time per iteration. Possibly increase the batch size over time.
3 · Step sizeBoth the size and the schedule affect speed and solution quality. Possibly decrease the step over time.
4 · TerminationStopping early avoids the long tail of slow progress. In machine learning, early stopping also acts as regularization.

Summary

  • Mini-batches average \(B\) stochastic gradients, which reduces noise.
  • \(B = 1\) is SGD, \(B = n\) is gradient descent, and mini-batches sit in between.
  • In practice, tune the initialization, batch size, step-size schedule and stopping rule.
How this shows up on the exam

Last year's midterm asked whether SGD with mini-batches gives the same solution as plain SGD. It need not: plain SGD's higher variance can escape local minima that a mini-batch method, behaving more like gradient descent, gets stuck in.

It also asked to rank gradient descent, Newton's method and SGD on a regression problem. Cost per iteration: SGD cheapest, then gradient descent, then Newton. Number of iterations: SGD most, then gradient descent, then Newton fewest. And to match contour plots: the noisy path is SGD, the direct one Newton, the steady zigzag gradient descent.

Check yourself
Quick check
A dataset has \(n = 1{,}000\) points. Roughly how many mini-batch iterations with \(B = 50\) cost the same number of gradient evaluations as one gradient descent iteration?
One GD iteration evaluates 1,000 component gradients. Each mini-batch iteration evaluates 50. So 20 mini-batch iterations, one epoch, cost the same.
