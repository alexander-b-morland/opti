Here and now, wait and see

Some decisions must be made before the uncertainty is resolved. Others can wait. Adaptive optimization chooses the first kind while planning how the second kind will respond in every scenario.

Two kinds of decisions

Here and now (first stage)

Strategic decisions made early, before the uncertainty is known. They cannot be changed once information arrives. Example: which facilities to build.

Wait and see (second stage)

Tactical decisions made later, once the uncertainty is resolved. They should be postponed until then. Example: how to route products once demand is known.

A good strategic decision must anticipate how operations will unfold in each possible future. Building few facilities is cheap now but makes shortages likely later. Building many is expensive now but makes operations easy. The applications are everywhere: supply chains, emergency services, power systems.

How this differs from robust optimization

Classes 13 and 14 made one decision that had to work in the worst case. Here, uncertainty is a list of scenarios with probabilities, some decisions adapt after the uncertainty is revealed, and we minimize expected cost.

Reminder: facility location

There are \(n\) candidate facilities, with construction cost \(c_j\) and capacity \(C_j\), and \(m\) customers with demand \(d_i\), transport cost \(t_{ij}\) and a penalty \(u_i\) per unit of unmet demand. The decisions are

  • \(x_j \in \{0, 1\}\): build facility \(j\) or not,
  • \(y_{ij} \ge 0\): flow from facility \(j\) to customer \(i\),
  • \(z_i \ge 0\): unmet demand of customer \(i\).
\[ \begin{aligned} \min\ & \sum_{j} c_j x_j + \sum_{j}\sum_{i} t_{ij} y_{ij} + \sum_{i} u_i z_i \\ \text{s.t. } & \sum_i y_{ij} \le C_j x_j, && j = 1, \dots, n \\ & \sum_j y_{ij} + z_i \ge d_i, && i = 1, \dots, m \\ & y, z \ge 0,\ x \in \{0, 1\}^n. \end{aligned} \]

The issue: the demand \(d_i\) is uncertain when facilities are built.

Adaptive facility location

Describe demand by \(S\) scenarios. Scenario \(s\) has probability \(\pi_s\) and demands \(d_{is}\). Now split the decisions by when they are made:

  • First stage: \(x_j\), one value for all scenarios.
  • Second stage: \(y_{ijs}\) and \(z_{is}\), a separate value for each scenario.
\[ \begin{aligned} \min\ & \sum_{j} c_j x_j + \sum_{s=1}^S \pi_s \Big( \sum_j \sum_i t_{ij} y_{ijs} + \sum_i u_i z_{is} \Big) \\ \text{s.t. } & \sum_i y_{ijs} \le C_j x_j, && j = 1, \dots, n,\ s = 1, \dots, S \\ & \sum_j y_{ijs} + z_{is} \ge d_{is}, && i = 1, \dots, m,\ s = 1, \dots, S \\ & y, z \ge 0,\ x \in \{0, 1\}^n. \end{aligned} \]
  • The objective is the construction cost (certain) plus the expected operating cost over the scenarios.
  • Capacity and demand constraints must hold in every scenario.
  • The \(x_j\) appear in every scenario's constraints. That coupling is what makes the first-stage choice hard.

The general two-stage model

\[ \begin{aligned} \min\ & c^\top x + \mathbb{E}\big[f_s^\top y_s\big] \\ \text{s.t. } & Ax = b \\ & U_s x + V y_s = w_s, && s \in \mathcal{S} \\ & x \in \mathbb{Z}_+^r \times \mathbb{R}_+^{n_1 - r},\ \ y_s \ge 0. \end{aligned} \]

\(x\) is the first-stage decision (possibly with integer parts). \(y_s\) is the second-stage decision in scenario \(s\). The data \(f_s, U_s, w_s\) may differ by scenario.

\[ \begin{array}{rcccccl} \min & c^\top x & + \pi_1 f_1^\top y_1 & + \pi_2 f_2^\top y_2 & \cdots & + \pi_S f_S^\top y_S & \\ \text{s.t.} & Ax & & & & & = b \\ & U_1 x & + V y_1 & & & & = w_1 \\ & U_2 x & & + V y_2 & & & = w_2 \\ & \vdots & & & \ddots & & \vdots \\ & U_S x & & & & + V y_S & = w_S \end{array} \]

This is a block-angular matrix. Each scenario has its own block of "nice" second-stage variables, and the first-stage \(x\) is the only thing linking them. Remove \(x\) and the problem splits into \(S\) small independent problems. Part b exploits exactly this.

  • Non-anticipativity: first-stage decisions cannot depend on which scenario happens. They are scenario-agnostic.
  • Recourse: second-stage decisions are the corrective actions taken after the scenario is revealed. They are scenario-dependent.
  • Size: \(S\) times more second-stage variables and constraints than the deterministic model. A large mixed-integer problem.

A one-facility example

To see the trade-off, shrink the model to one facility. Build capacity \(x\) now at cost 1 per unit. Then demand turns out to be 40, 100 or 160 (probabilities 0.35, 0.3, 0.35), and each unit of unmet demand costs \(u\). The expected cost is

\[ x + Q(x), \qquad Q(x) = u \sum_s \pi_s \max(d_s - x,\ 0). \]

With \(u = 4\), it pays to cover even the high-demand scenario. With \(u = 2\), it does not, and the two-stage plan coincides with the plan for average demand.

Recipe: from deterministic to two-stage

Exam questions often give a deterministic model and ask for its adaptive version. Four steps:

  1. Sort the variables. Which decisions are made before the uncertainty is revealed (first stage) and which after (second stage)?
  2. Index the second-stage variables by scenario. \(y_{ij}\) becomes \(y_{ijs}\). First-stage variables keep no scenario index.
  3. Take the expectation in the objective. First-stage costs once, second-stage costs weighted by \(\pi_s\) and summed over \(s\).
  4. Copy every constraint that involves second-stage variables for each scenario, with the scenario's data (\(d_{is}\), \(p_{ks}\)). Constraints with only first-stage variables stay as they are.
How this shows up on the exam

Last year's Midterm 2 gave a vaccine manufacturing model with leased bioreactor lines (binary), reactor-days per antigen and liters per product, then said that profits and demand are uncertain and that lines must be leased before prices are known. The answer applies the recipe: leasing stays first stage, production variables get an index \(s\), the objective becomes \(\frac{1}{S}\sum_s \sum_k p_{ks} z_{ks}\), and every production constraint is repeated for each \(s\).

The practice exam did the same for police patrol vehicles: deploy before an event (first stage), reassign to incidents after (second stage).

Summary

  • First-stage decisions are made here and now, are the same in every scenario, and cannot anticipate the future.
  • Second-stage decisions wait and see, and get a copy for each scenario.
  • Minimize first-stage cost plus expected second-stage cost, with second-stage constraints in every scenario.
  • The constraint matrix is block-angular: the first stage couples otherwise independent scenario blocks.
