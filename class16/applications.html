Inventory and recruiting

Two classic applications. In both, the optimal policy turns out to have a simple shape, and the shape itself is the managerial insight.

Inventory control: the model

Each month you decide how many units to order. Units sell at price \(p\). Ordering costs a fixed \(F\) plus \(c\) per unit. Holding stock costs \(h\) per unit at the start of each month. Demand is uncertain, and unmet demand is lost (no backlog).

IngredientInventory model
State\(x_k\): inventory at the start of month \(k\)
Decision\(u_k\): amount ordered at the start of month \(k\)
Uncertainty\(d_k\): demand during month \(k\)
Transition\(x_{k+1} = \max(x_k + u_k - d_k,\ 0)\)
Units sold\(x_k + u_k - x_{k+1} = \min(d_k,\ x_k + u_k)\)

The reward (to be maximized) in month \(k\) is

\[ g_k(x_k, u_k) = \begin{cases} p \min(d_k, x_k + u_k) - h x_k & \text{if } u_k = 0, \\ p \min(d_k, x_k + u_k) - h x_k - F - c\,u_k & \text{if } u_k > 0, \end{cases} \qquad g_N(x_N) = -h x_N. \]

The fixed cost \(F\) makes the problem non-linear and non-convex: ordering one unit costs almost as much as ordering many. Dynamic programming does not mind.

The order-up-to policy

Interactive

The optimal order for each inventory level

Each bar is the optimal order \(u_k = \mu_k(x_k)\) in the chosen month, computed by backward induction. Look at the shape, then raise and lower the fixed cost.

Two patterns appear in every month, and also in the lecture's own table of optimal orders:

  • With low inventory, the order falls one-for-one as inventory rises (a slope of \(-1\)). You always order up to the same level \(S_k\).
  • With high inventory, you do not order at all.
Order-up-to policy \((s_k, S_k)\)
\[ \mu_k(x_k) = \begin{cases} S_k - x_k & \text{if } x_k < s_k, \\ 0 & \text{if } x_k \ge s_k. \end{cases} \]

The gap between \(s_k\) and \(S_k\) comes from the fixed cost. With \(F = 0\) you would top up every month. With a large \(F\), you wait until stock is low and then order a lot at once. Near the end of the horizon the levels drop, because leftover stock only costs holding fees.

In the lecture's example, a run looks like this: start with 0 units in month 1 and order 29. Demand is 14, so month 2 starts with 15: no order. Demand is 12, so month 3 starts with 3: order 25. And so on.

Recruiting: the model

You interview \(n\) candidates one at a time. Each scores between 0 and 100. After each interview you either hire that candidate (and stop) or reject them forever. When should you make an offer?

IngredientRecruiting model
State\(x_k\): score of candidate \(k\), or \(T\) once someone has been hired
Decision\(u_k = 1\) to hire candidate \(k\), \(u_k = 0\) to continue
Uncertainty\(s_k\): the score of candidate \(k\)
Transition\(x_{k+1} = T\) if \(x_k = T\) or \(u_k = 1\). Otherwise \(x_{k+1} = s_{k+1}\).
Bellman's equation
\[ J_k(x_k) = \max\big\{ x_k,\ \mathbb{E}[J_{k+1}(s_{k+1})] \big\}, \qquad J_{n+1}(\cdot) = 0,\qquad J_k(T) = 0. \]

Hire now and get the score \(x_k\), or continue and get the expected value of the rest of the process.

Falling thresholds

The equation says: hire candidate \(k\) exactly when their score beats \(\mathbb{E}[J_{k+1}(s_{k+1})]\), the value of continuing. That value is a number, so the policy is a threshold for each interview slot.

Interactive

Minimum score needed to be hired

Scores are uniform on 0 to 100. The bars show the hiring threshold for each interview slot under the optimal policy.

For recruiters

Become less demanding over time. Early on, many candidates remain, so continuing is valuable. The last candidate is hired whatever their score.

For applicants

If you are strong, interview early, before the firm has settled. If you are weaker, interview late, when the bar has dropped.

Summary

  • Inventory with a fixed ordering cost: the optimal policy is order-up-to, \((s_k, S_k)\).
  • Recruiting: hire when the score beats the expected value of continuing. The threshold falls over time.
  • In both, solving the DP numerically reveals a simple policy structure that managers can use directly.
Check yourself
Quick check
The policy in a month is \(s_k = 7\), \(S_k = 29\). You start the month with 4 units. How much do you order?
4 is below the reorder point 7, so order up to 29: \(29 - 4 = 25\).
