Convergence guarantees

Gradient descent works, but how well? Two assumptions about the shape of \(f\) turn vague hope into exact iteration counts. Both come down to squeezing \(f\) between two parabolas.

Two shape assumptions

Without assumptions, nothing useful can be said: \(f\) could have a cliff right next to a flat plain, and no fixed step size would suit both. The lecture uses two standard conditions.

Definition · smoothness

\(f\) is \(M\)-smooth if its gradient is \(M\)-Lipschitz continuous:

\[ \|\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\| \le M \|x - y\| \quad \text{for all } x, y. \]

The gradient cannot change too quickly, so the function cannot bend upward too sharply. For a twice differentiable \(f\) this is the same as \(\nabla^2 f(x) \preceq M I\) everywhere: no eigenvalue of the Hessian exceeds \(M\). Smoothness is an upper limit on curvature.

Definition · strong convexity

\(f\) is \(m\)-strongly convex if

\[ f(x) - \frac{m}{2}\|x\|^2 \quad \text{is convex.} \]

Even after you subtract a bowl of curvature \(m\), what remains still curves upward. For a twice differentiable \(f\) this means \(\nabla^2 f(x) \succeq m I\) everywhere: every eigenvalue of the Hessian is at least \(m\). Strong convexity is a lower limit on curvature, and rules out flat regions.

In one sentence

The curvature of \(f\), in every direction and at every point, lies between \(m\) and \(M\). For a quadratic \(f(x) = \tfrac12 x^\top Q x - c^\top x\), these are just \(m = \lambda_{\min}(Q)\) and \(M = \lambda_{\max}(Q)\).

The quadratic sandwich

The lecture states what the two definitions imply. These are the two inequalities the convergence proof uses:

Upper bound from \(M\)-smoothness
\[ f(y) \le f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x) + \frac{M}{2}\|y - x\|^2 \quad \text{for all } x, y. \]
Lower bound from \(m\)-strong convexity
\[ f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x) + \frac{m}{2}\|y - x\|^2 \quad \text{for all } x, y. \]

Stand at any point \(x\). Draw the tangent line, then bend it up by \(\tfrac{M}{2}\|y - x\|^2\) and by \(\tfrac{m}{2}\|y - x\|^2\). The function is trapped between the two parabolas everywhere. Plain convexity is the case \(m = 0\): the tangent itself is the lower bound.

The widget shows why step size \(1/M\) is the natural choice. The upper parabola is a simple quadratic model of \(f\), and its minimizer is exactly

\[ \arg\min_y \Big\{ f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x) + \frac{M}{2}\|y - x\|^2 \Big\} = x - \frac{1}{M}\nabla f(x). \]

So gradient descent with \(\alpha = 1/M\) means: minimize the upper bound, and move there. Since \(f\) lies below the bound, \(f\) drops at least as much as the bound promises.

Rate under convexity: sub-linear

Theorem (convex and smooth)

Let \(f\) be \(M\)-smooth and convex. Gradient descent with constant step size \(\alpha = 1/M\) satisfies

\[ f(x_k) - z^* \le \frac{1}{2\alpha k}\|x_0 - x^*\|^2 = \frac{M\|x_0 - x^*\|^2}{2k}, \qquad k \ge 1. \]
  • The gap shrinks like a constant divided by \(k\), so the convergence is sub-linear.
  • To guarantee \(f(x_k) - z^* \le \varepsilon\), it is enough to run \(k \ge \dfrac{\|x_0 - x^*\|^2}{2\alpha\varepsilon}\) iterations, that is \(O(1/\varepsilon)\).
  • A poor start (large \(\|x_0 - x^*\|\)) and a tight tolerance (small \(\varepsilon\)) both cost iterations in direct proportion.

Rate under strong convexity: linear

Theorem (strongly convex and smooth)

Let \(f\) be \(M\)-smooth and \(m\)-strongly convex. Gradient descent with constant step size \(\alpha = 1/M\) satisfies

\[ f(x_k) - z^* \le \left(1 - \frac{m}{M}\right)^k \big(f(x_0) - z^*\big), \qquad k \ge 0. \]
  • The gap is multiplied by at most \(c = 1 - m/M < 1\) at every step: linear convergence.
  • Accuracy \(\varepsilon\) takes \(O\!\left(\tfrac{M}{m}\log\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\) iterations. Tightening the tolerance from \(10^{-3}\) to \(10^{-6}\) only doubles the work, while in the sub-linear case it multiplies it by 1,000.
  • The ratio \(M/m\) now matters a lot. If \(M/m\) is large, \(c\) is close to 1 and each step barely helps.

The proof, step by step

This proof is short and uses only the two sandwich inequalities, so it is worth knowing by heart.

Progress per step, from smoothness. Plug \(y = x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha\nabla f(x_k)\) into the upper bound:

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x_{k+1}) &\le f(x_k) + \nabla f(x_k)^\top (x_{k+1} - x_k) + \tfrac{M}{2}\|x_{k+1} - x_k\|^2 \\ &= f(x_k) - \alpha\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 + \tfrac{M\alpha^2}{2}\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 \\ &= f(x_k) - \tfrac{1}{2M}\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 \qquad (\alpha = 1/M). \end{aligned} \]

In words: each step lowers \(f\) by at least \(\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2/(2M)\). A large gradient guarantees a large decrease.

How far from optimal can we be, from strong convexity. The lower bound holds for every \(y\). Minimize both sides over \(y\). The left side becomes \(z^*\). The right side is a parabola in \(y\), minimized at \(y = x_k - \tfrac{1}{m}\nabla f(x_k)\):

\[ z^* \ge f(x_k) - \frac{1}{2m}\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2. \]

Rearranged: \(\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 \ge 2m\,(f(x_k) - z^*)\). In words: if you are far from optimal in value, the gradient must be large. Strong convexity rules out long, nearly flat approaches to the minimum.

Chain the two. Subtract \(z^*\) from step 1 and insert step 2:

\[ f(x_{k+1}) - z^* \le f(x_k) - z^* - \frac{1}{2M}\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 \le \left(1 - \frac{m}{M}\right)(f(x_k) - z^*). \]

Apply this \(k\) times (telescoping) to get \(f(x_k) - z^* \le (1 - m/M)^k (f(x_0) - z^*)\).

The logic in one line: a big gap forces a big gradient (step 2), and a big gradient forces a big decrease (step 1).

What drives the speed

In the linear rate, the function enters only through the ratio \(M/m\) of the largest to the smallest curvature. \(M/m = 1\) means equal curvature in every direction, and then \(c = 0\). A large \(M/m\) puts \(c = 1 - m/M\) close to 1, and each step removes only a small share of the gap.

The lecture lists four drivers of performance:

DriverEffect
Initial point \(x_0\)The further from \(x^*\), the more iterations. Enters through \(\|x_0 - x^*\|^2\) or \(f(x_0) - z^*\).
Step size \(\alpha\)Larger is faster, but not too large: the "right" choice of \(\alpha\) is what makes convergence work. Part d.
Tolerance \(\varepsilon\)Smaller is slower: \(1/\varepsilon\) in the convex case, \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\) in the strongly convex case.
The function \(f\)Through \(c = 1 - m/M\). A large ratio \(M/m\) means slow convergence.

Ridge regression illustrates the last row. The Hessian is \(X^\top X + \lambda I\), so \(m = \lambda_{\min}(X^\top X) + \lambda\) and \(M = \lambda_{\max}(X^\top X) + \lambda\). Increasing \(\lambda\) raises both by the same amount, which pushes \(M/m\) towards 1. More regularization lowers \(c\), and gradient descent gets faster.

Summary

  • \(M\)-smooth: curvature at most \(M\), quadratic upper bound. \(m\)-strongly convex: curvature at least \(m\), quadratic lower bound.
  • Step size \(1/M\) minimizes the upper bound at each step.
  • Convex and \(M\)-smooth: \(f(x_k) - z^* \le M\|x_0 - x^*\|^2/(2k)\). Sub-linear, \(O(1/\varepsilon)\).
  • Also \(m\)-strongly convex: \(f(x_k) - z^* \le (1 - m/M)^k (f(x_0) - z^*)\). Linear, \(O\big(\tfrac{M}{m}\log(1/\varepsilon)\big)\).
How this shows up on the exam

Expect to compare two guarantees. If an algorithm satisfies \(f(x_{k+1}) - z^* \le (1 - \gamma\, m/M)(f(x_k) - z^*)\) with \(0 < \gamma < 1\), its rate is still linear, because the limit of the ratio is a constant below 1. But its constant is larger than \(1 - m/M\), so the guarantee is weaker than plain gradient descent with \(\alpha = 1/M\).

You may also be asked to reproduce the three-step proof above, or to say which rate applies when strong convexity is missing.

Check yourself
Quick check
\(f(x) = 2x_1^2 + 8x_2^2\). What are the smallest valid \(M\) and the largest valid \(m\)?
The Hessian is \(\mathrm{diag}(4, 16)\), so the curvature ranges from 4 to 16. Careful with the factor 2: \(\partial^2 (2x_1^2)/\partial x_1^2 = 4\). So \(M/m = 4\).
Quick check
With \(m = 1\) and \(M = 100\), roughly how many iterations does the linear guarantee need to cut the gap by a factor \(e \approx 2.7\)?
\((1 - 1/100)^k \approx e^{-k/100}\), so \(k \approx 100 = M/m\) iterations per factor of \(e\). This is where \(O\big(\tfrac{M}{m} \log(1/\varepsilon)\big)\) comes from.
